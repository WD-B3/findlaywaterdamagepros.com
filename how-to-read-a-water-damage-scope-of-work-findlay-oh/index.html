How to Read a Water Damage Scope of Work in Findlay, OH

The scope of work is the most important page you will be handed. Here is a sample layout and how to check it against what you saw.

☎ (567) 406-9153

A scope of work is a written list of what a contractor will do, where and with what. In water damage jobs it is the bridge between what you saw, what the crew found and what your insurer will review. Signing without reading it is the easiest mistake to make, and the one most likely to cause an argument later.

Below is a sample layout, section by section. The details will differ by company. The structure should look familiar, and any section that is missing is worth asking about.

A sample scope, section by section

Compare the scope with what you saw

Walk through the house with the document. If it says baseboards were removed in the hallway, are they gone? If it lists a dehumidifier in the basement, is it there? This is not suspicion. It is how an honest scope gets confirmed, and a good crew expects it.

Photos taken on day one help. They show the starting condition and let you see what was removed and why.

Watch for vague wording

  • Remove damaged materials as needed, with no list.
  • Dry structure, with no equipment count or duration.
  • Clean and sanitize, with no mention of surfaces.
  • Rebuild to pre-loss condition, with no itemization.
  • Any clause that lets the price change without your approval.

Vague scopes are hard to compare and hard to defend with an insurer. Ask for specifics in writing and keep the amended version. Vague wording also makes a claim harder to document, so ask for photos and moisture readings to be attached to the scope.

Pay attention to the end of the job

A good scope states when drying is complete, such as moisture readings returning to the dry standard for the materials. It also says what happens after, whether that is a final visit, a certificate of completion or a handoff to a rebuild contractor. Make sure you know who is responsible for the next stage.

A short example of a clear line item

A vague line says dry the structure. A clear one says that in the hall and bedroom, six air movers and two dehumidifiers will run for an estimated four days, with moisture readings recorded each visit until the drywall and framing reach the dry target. The second version can be checked, compared and defended.

The same goes for removal. A clear line names the material, the room and the reason, such as removing the lower two feet of wet drywall and the baseboard in the hall because readings showed moisture above the dry standard. Ask the crew to show you the reading that justified each cut.

Keep the scope with the claim file

Store the signed scope with your photos, receipts and the insurer's claim number. If anything changes, ask for an updated scope or a written change note. Over a long job, the paper trail is what keeps everyone's memory aligned.

Questions to ask when you get the scope

  • Which items are mitigation and which are repairs?
  • What readings will show that drying is complete?
  • Who decides if more materials need to be removed, and how will I be told?
  • What happens if the drying takes longer than planned?
  • What is excluded from this scope?

The last question matters most. Exclusions are where surprises come from. If the scope says nothing about the plumbing repair, the paint or the flooring, it is worth confirming that each belongs to someone else.

Findlay note: know before you dig

If any part of the repair involves digging outdoors, such as exposing a foundation wall or a leaking line, Ohio law has a call-before-you-dig step. The Ohio Utilities Protection Service takes 811 or 1-800-362-2764 calls to mark buried utilities, and the City of Findlay's water page lists the same number. A scope that includes excavation should say who makes that call.

For permit questions on rebuild work, the City Zoning Office is the starting point and Wood County Building Inspection covers multi-family and commercial properties in Findlay and Hancock County, according to the city's zoning page. See also the basement page for scopes that involve below-grade rooms. Properties in Arlington and McComb should check with their own village office.

A local question answered

Should I sign a scope before work starts?

Read it first and ask for any vague lines to be itemized. You should understand what you are approving before work begins.

What happens if the scope changes?

A clear scope states how changes are approved. Ask that any price change be agreed in writing first.

Who calls 811 in Ohio if excavation is needed?

The scope should say. The Ohio Utilities Protection Service handles 811 and 1-800-362-2764 calls to mark buried lines before digging.
