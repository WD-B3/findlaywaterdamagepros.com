How to Save Wet Photos and Documents After Water Damage in Findlay, OH

Family photos and paperwork are the things people ask about first. The National Archives has clear guidance, and the first two days matter most.

☎ (567) 406-9153

When water reaches a closet shelf or a basement box, the first worry is rarely the drywall. It is the wedding album, the deed, the child's drawings. The good news is that wet paper and photographs are often recoverable if they are handled correctly and quickly. The bad news is that the most common instinct, which is to pile everything together to deal with later, makes things worse.

This guide follows the National Archives' public guidance on flood-damaged family papers and photographs. It is general information. For items of high monetary, historic or sentimental value, the Archives recommends consulting a conservator before you do anything irreversible.

The two-day rule

The National Archives advises that wet materials should be air dried within about two days. Mold can begin to grow on wet paper quickly, so if you cannot dry items in that window, the guidance is to freeze them, because freezing inhibits mold growth and buys you time.

A household freezer is enough for a few items. For a large volume, a conservator or a document recovery service may offer freezing and vacuum drying.

That window is why the first call on a flooded home often involves two separate questions. One is how to dry the building. The other is what to do with the boxes on the shelf. Both are time-sensitive, and the contents question is easy to forget.

Drying without damaging

Lay items flat on clean blotting paper or unprinted newsprint, and replace it as it soaks up water. Use fans to move air in the room, but do not point them directly at the items, since strong airflow can curl and tear paper. Keep the room as cool and dry as you can with a dehumidifier.

Do not use a hair dryer, an oven or direct sunlight. Heat can warp photographs, and it can set stains permanently. Patience is the tool here.

When a conservator is the right call

Some items cannot be replaced, and some are worth more than the cost of expert help. A conservator can stabilize and treat damaged works in ways that home methods cannot. The American Institute for Conservation maintains a directory, and the Archives suggests contacting one for valuable or historic material.

For valuable documents without active mold, freezing first and consulting afterward is a sound sequence. It stops the clock without causing damage.

Protect the contents while the building is dried

Restoration crews often pack out contents, meaning they move belongings to a clean area so that drying equipment can run without obstruction. Ask whether boxes of papers can be set aside so they are not lost in the pile.

Your adjuster may also want a list of what was damaged, so keep an inventory. The structural drying page explains how the building is dried so that new contents are not damaged again.

A first-day checklist

  1. Wear gloves, since floodwater can be contaminated.
  2. Move wet items to a clean, flat, ventilated space.
  3. Separate photos, papers and books so they do not stick together.
  4. Freeze what you cannot dry within two days, except old photographs and negatives.
  5. Photograph the items before and after, and list them for your insurer.

Write a short label for each bag or box going into the freezer. When you finally have time to sort it out, you will want to know which box holds the deed and which one holds the school photos.

A local question answered

Can wet photos be saved?

Often, yes, if you handle them quickly. Modern photos can be rinsed gently and air dried. Old photographs and negatives should not be frozen, so ask a conservator.

Should I freeze wet books?

If you cannot air dry them within about two days, freezing inhibits mold growth according to the National Archives. Do not freeze old photographs or negatives.

Is there help for valuable family records in Hancock County?

Ask a library or archive near you whether it can point you to a conservator. A professional conservator is the right call for items of high value.
